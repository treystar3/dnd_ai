# Let places, not characters, keep time

Shared worlds deal with players who move through time at different speeds in nine recognisable ways, and nearly all of them either forbid long skips, make the whole group agree to them, or hand each player a private copy of the world. Only three (the time track, a clock per player, a clock per place) cope with a one-line two-day walk, and no shipped game or published system combines any of them with narration that cannot be taken back. The current proposal (design docs 06 and 07) is, in established terms, timestamp ordering with a throttle: it guarantees that nothing is done in anything's past, but only for things that are acted on, and the notes identify eight places where it is unsound or silent, the worst being a character who arrives ahead of someone still playing an earlier hour in the same town. Theory also says the two-day window is not what keeps the story consistent and offers no way to choose its size; the real choice hiding behind it is who gives way when clocks meet: the one ahead waits in real time, or the one behind is carried forward in game time. This report recommends a hybrid, **place clocks with agreed skips**: one clock per place (the unit the world simulation already catches up lazily), travel that brings clocks level in the forward direction only, long skips in a shared place offered to the people present instead of imposed on them, and the window turned from a wall into a warning. It needs less stored state than the repaired proposal, makes "two players in one scene see the same world" true by construction, contains no quantity that depends on the number of players, and keeps a strict setting in reserve for the day players may fight each other. The recommendation rests on inference at three points, flagged where they occur: no evidence was found on how players feel about being asked versus being moved, none on waiting in a narrated multiplayer game, and none for any of the four small numbers the owner would have to choose.

## Nine time models exist, and only three survive a two-day walk

The six sets of research notes cover online worlds, co-op role-playing games, survival sandboxes, strategy and play-by-mail games, tabletop campaigns, text role-play communities, products narrated by language models, and the theory of simulated time. Sorted by what they do with the clock, they fall into nine models. Docs 06 and 07 already cover several of the examples (AD&D time records, West Marches, Baldur's Gate 3, Crusader Kings III, the DikuMUD and LPMud families, play-by-post flashbacks, World of Warcraft phasing, EVE time dilation); those are placed in the table below without being surveyed again.

### Models that forbid skips or make the group own them

The first three models never let two players sit at different game times, which is why they never contradict themselves and why none of them fits as the main design. The wall-clock model works only because every action takes as long as it takes: EverQuest ran one shared clock at 72 real minutes to the game day (documented by a classic-era emulator wiki, seen as a search summary only) ([Project 1999 wiki](https://wiki.project1999.com/Time)), and one blogger's account, with his own estimates, concludes that its enforced waiting lost the market to a game that cut downtime by a factor of three to five ([Tobold's blog](https://tobolds.blogspot.com/2006/06/changes-part-3-time.html?m=1)). The tabletop revival of this model locks any character whose date has run ahead until the real calendar catches up, and its critics' strongest complaint is not bookkeeping but being unable to play ([theRPGsite](https://therpgsite.com/pen-paper-roleplaying-games-rpgs-discussion/don-t-fall-for-the-strict-timekeeping-fad/45)). Text role-play communities that fix a ratio of game time to real time report that long scenes drift days out of step and late joiners assume the wrong time of day; the one editorial that compares ratios concludes that none is best ([AresMUSH](https://aresmush.com/articles/ic-vs-rl-time.html)).

The agreed-skip model has the clearest arc in the evidence: games launch with unanimity, players complain that one person blocks the night, and the rule is relaxed. **Minecraft counts only players in the Overworld toward a sleep and now lets the server set the share required** ([Minecraft Wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Bed)); Valheim's all-in-bed rule has at least six community replacements, one of which added a timeout specifically so that idle players cannot block ([SleepSkip changelog](https://thunderstore.io/c/valheim/p/Azumatt/SleepSkip/changelog)); Stardew Valley forces the issue by making everyone pass out at 2am (search summary of the wiki) ([Stardew Valley Wiki](https://www.stardewvalleywiki.com/Day_Cycle)). Baldur's Gate 3 has no time skip except the long rest, which gathers and requires every player; reports of the consent screen hanging are forum posts only ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1086940/discussions/0/6292161380820233297)). Two details transfer: scoping the vote to those in the place the skip affects, and counting silence as agreement after a timeout.

Fixed turns with a deadline and a default are the best evidenced answer to silence. A hobby writer reports that every one of ten online Diplomacy games he played was damaged by a departing player (anecdotal, but consistent with the house rules every league adopts) ([Diplomatic Pouch](https://diplom.org/Zine/S2013R/Hartl/nmrcd.htm)); play-by-post referees post a deadline and assume a defensive default for anyone who misses it ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/goto/post?id=7089669)); and a designer of multiplayer interactive fiction advises never to trigger an event on every player having acted, because players leave or idle ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2015/05/05/writing-for-seltani-an-aspel-post-mortem/)). The owner's decision to make "wait for everyone or move on after a window" a host setting is this lesson already taken. What the model cannot do is price a six-second action and a two-day action on the same scale; published systems that use a coarse step (a season, a year, a month-long downtime turn) simply decline to order anything inside it.

### Models that give out separate clocks

The time track is the only board-game mechanic that prices every action in time and guarantees nobody acts in a resolved past: each action advances only the actor, and whoever is furthest back acts next, repeatedly if they stay last ([Meeple Mountain on Thebes](https://www.meeplemountain.com/articles/games-we-love-thebes-you-and-me-going-digging-by-the-sea/)). Red November is a co-operative example in minutes, with world events attached to points on the track ([Hiew's Boardgame Blog](http://hiewandboardgames.blogspot.com/2009/09/red-november.html)). Its costs are a long real wait after a long action and a stall when the earliest player goes silent. Role-playing games that used the same idea for combat drew three complaints (bookkeeping, a mechanical feel, fast characters acting several times while slow ones sat idle), all from forum posts ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/goto/post?id=4683864)); the first two disappear when an engine keeps the count. Complaints about the board-game mechanic itself were not found, and the notes say the usual forum for them was not retrieved.

A clock per player with forward-only synchronisation is what the Kerbal Space Program multiplayer mods built, and it is the nearest shipped relative of the current proposal. A player who skips ahead enters a private time state; others can still see and talk to them; **the only way to interact is for the one behind to jump forward, and craft from another time state are shown translucent and cannot be affected** ([KMP README](https://github.com/TehGimp/KerbalMultiPlayer/blob/master/README.md)). The successor mod stores each player's time as a shared base plus an offset, gives up rewind entirely, and disabled the contract types that depend on shared, time-evolving objects because they could not be kept consistent ([Luna Multiplayer wiki](https://github.com/LunaMultiplayer/LunaMultiplayer/wiki/Limitations)). The developer's own admissions of leftover paradoxes (a player in the past driving through one in the future) are known only from search summaries of forum posts, as is the player criticism that others pop in and out of existence ([KSP forum](https://forum.kerbalspaceprogram.com/topic/187358-ksp-2-multiplayer-discussion-thread/page/62)). The lesson is specific: vessels were locked across time, places and bystanders were not, and shared objectives were removed. The one tabletop campaign found with a date per character adds two rules worth keeping: a lagging character may spend the gap forward as downtime up to the most advanced character's date and never past it, and nothing may be ordered retroactively, because a cost only means something if it is paid before the outcome is known ([Erin's campaign notes](https://erin1478.blogspot.com/2025/12/campaign-news-december-17.html)). Its referee calls the bookkeeping a heavy load that will eventually cause an error, which is the part an engine removes.

A clock per place is what the RimWorld multiplayer mod calls async time, and the notes read its source directly. Every map carries its own tick counter, speed and random-number state; a new map starts at the latest clock among existing maps, never in another map's past ([MapSetup.cs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rwmt/Multiplayer/master/Source/Client/Patches/MapSetup.cs)). The files read contain no code that reconciles two maps or handles anything crossing between them, and the bug list is a catalogue of exactly those crossings: quests, cooldowns, caravans, and a pause flag written while one map's time was temporarily swapped in for the global one, a bug open since November 2022 ([issue 339](https://github.com/rwmt/Multiplayer/issues/339)). Players in a 2023 thread warned each other not to use the feature at all (forum only) ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/294100/discussions/0/3764479392870416354)). The structure is right for this project; what is missing is the rule for crossing.

### Models that dodge the question, and the field nearest to this project

Private copies of the world solve crowding, not time, and their documented cost is the opposite of this project's goal: grouped friends at different story stages stop seeing each other in The Elder Scrolls Online (forum only) ([ESO forum](https://forums.elderscrollsonline.com/en/discussion/161407/friend-in-group-disappearing)), and World of Warcraft's per-character timelines left family members unable to play together (forum only) ([Blizzard forum](https://us.forums.blizzard.com/en/wow/t/i-cant-play-with-my-lower-level-family-members-stuck-in-chromie-time/1972491)). Time as a budget produced lockout: Living Greyhawk gave each character 52 time units a year, made a character with none left unplayable until January, and stopped charging adventures for time in its final year ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_Greyhawk)). Rolling back is ruled out by its own requirements: the standard rollback library needs a fully deterministic, fully saveable state whose output is a picture replaced sixty times a second ([GGPO developer guide](https://github.com/pond3r/ggpo/blob/master/doc/DeveloperGuide.md)), and even in a shooter the repair shows as players returning to life ([Cronin et al.](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~bleong/hydra/related/cronin03efficient.pdf)).

Products narrated by a language model offer almost nothing to copy. **None that could be read documents an in-fiction clock kept by code.** AI Dungeon resolves several players in arrival order and its own help page warns of overlapping actions ([AI Dungeon help](https://help.aidungeon.com/faq/do-you-support-multiplayer)); Friends and Fables removed its "everyone ready" gate in April 2025 in favour of letting the sender decide ([patch note](https://fables.gg/patch-notes/input-bar-redesign-player-chat-mode-toggles)), and its users, with time left to the model, ask for a timestamp on every message (search summary of a private feedback post) ([feedback post](https://feedback.fables.gg/p/time-flow-as-a-optional-feature)). The one architecture document that matches this project's "model calls before the transaction" rule is AI Town's, where slow model work runs outside the simulation step and may only submit inputs for the engine to validate ([AI Town architecture](https://github.com/a16z-infra/ai-town/blob/main/ARCHITECTURE.md)). No product was found in which each participant's own model generates part of one shared story, and no study was found of how long players will wait on someone else's narration; both are gaps in the evidence, not findings of absence.

Across all nine models the handling of an absent character converges on one answer that the owner has already chosen. Ultima Online removes a character at once only when the player logs out at an inn or house ([UO official](https://uo.com/?p=10697)); Haven and Hearth returns a character to a home point if the logout spot is no longer reachable ([Ring of Brodgar](https://ringofbrodgar.com/wiki/Hearth_Fire)); and where a logged-out body stays in the world, players complain about what was done to it ([Brand MU Day](https://brandmu.day/post/16258)). Walking an offline character to the nearest safe place and checking them out of the world, as doc 07 proposes, is the well-trodden path.

## The current proposal is timestamp ordering with a throttle, sound in one direction

In established terms the proposal is three known things stacked together. The touch rule is the clock rule Leslie Lamport published in 1978 for ordering events among computers that share no clock: whoever receives a message sets their clock to at least the sender's time ([Lamport 1978](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/time-clocks.pdf)). The proposal treats every person, place and thing as a silent participant, so touching something is receiving its message. The same rule is what database designers call timestamp ordering: every record remembers when it was last used, and a transaction that tries to write into a record's past is restarted with a later time ([Jefferson 1985](https://gdo-pdes-course-2014.llnl.gov/attachments/20776356/20907222.pdf)). Because the proposal checks before the action runs, the restart costs nothing but the character's time. The perceive rule is a read "as of" the reader's time from dated facts. The window is what simulation research calls a moving time window: nobody may run more than a fixed width past the slowest participant ([Jefferson, 2014 lecture](https://gdo-pdes-course-2014.llnl.gov/attachments/20776356/23363701.pdf)).

What this guarantees is real and worth keeping. If one event could have caused another through a chain of touches, the first is dated at or before the second, so the world's history sorted by game time is a history that could have happened, and everyone can reason about before and after with everyday intuition. The guarantee runs one way only: an earlier date does not mean a cause, and the scheme sees nothing that is not a touch.

The theory also explains why the design has so little room to manoeuvre. Simulation research has two ways to keep order. The cautious way (called conservative) makes whoever is ahead wait until nothing earlier can still arrive. The bold way (called optimistic) lets everyone run ahead and undoes mistakes afterward, but it never releases anything that cannot be recalled until it is certainly safe; Jefferson's examples are dispensing money and launching a missile ([Jefferson 1985](https://gdo-pdes-course-2014.llnl.gov/attachments/20776356/20907222.pdf)). Narration a player has read is exactly that kind of output, and the game cannot hold it back, because the player is waiting for it. That leaves **two and only two ways to make a narrated turn safe: wait until nothing earlier can arrive (the one ahead waits in real time), or date everything the narration relied on so that later arrivals fall after it (the one behind is moved forward in game time)**. The proposal uses the second everywhere and the first only at the window's edge. Neither cost can be engineered away; it can only be bounded and shared out fairly. Undoing remains available for engine state that nobody has yet been told about, which is why lazy regional catch-up is safe. This framing is the notes' inference from the cited theory, not a statement by any source.

### Eight places where the proposal is unsound or silent

The first gap is that reads are not stamped. Timestamp ordering needs a record of the last read as well as the last write, because a write dated before someone's later read makes that read false. If the player ahead was told on day 3 that the statue stands, a slower player must not smash it on day 1; yet the statue was only described, never touched. Being narrated a thing has to count as touching it.

The second gap is claims of absence. "The square is empty" or "no word from the north" reads a whole situation, not an object, and no per-object stamp protects it; databases call the same failure a phantom, and the related failure, where two actors each read what the other then changes, is known to slip past per-record checks ([summary of Berenson et al.](https://mwhittaker.github.io/papers/html/berenson1995critique.html)). The region time is the only stamp coarse enough to cover such claims, and it must move when a region is narrated, not only when it is acted in. Consequences arriving from elsewhere need the mirror rule: they must never land in a stretch of a place's time that has already been told.

The third gap is the hard direction of a meeting. The proposal has a rule for a character who arrives behind a place (they are moved forward) and none for one who arrives ahead of a player still there. Doc 06's `arrive` advances the region to the arriver's clock regardless, which simulates the town through hours its resident has not played, possibly through a fight still in progress. Theory names the only three resolutions: hold the arriver until the resident catches up, skip the resident forward to the arrival, or let the arriver see the departure now and the arrival later. Which to use is a fairness decision about who gives way.

The fourth gap is one town at two times. Region time is kept per settlement, and `act_at` takes the later of the actor's clock and the region's. A sleeper who wakes and acts moves the town eight hours on; a fighter in the same town's cellar is then eight hours behind the region and is pulled forward by their own next action, in the middle of the fight. Doc 06's worked example assumes the two are in different places.

The fifth gap is the window itself. It is not a safety rule: a character two days ahead can still be affected by a slower one, and consistency comes from touch and perceive. As a global minimum it lets the slowest of any number of players throttle everyone, and a returning player who chooses to play on from an old clock would pin it. Its width has no basis: Jefferson says outright that how to choose it is handled by trial and that he finds the lack of a theory unsatisfactory ([Jefferson, 2014 lecture](https://gdo-pdes-course-2014.llnl.gov/attachments/20776356/23363701.pdf)), and the one formal analysis found shows the costs of a wide window growing in proportion to its width ([Dickens et al. 1996](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~carl/btwyawns.pdf)). A flat two days also ignores distance: it is too loose for two characters an hour apart, who can be made to jump two days on meeting, and needlessly tight for two a week apart.

The sixth gap is the skipped span. In a simulator a clock that jumps skipped nothing; a character has a life in the interval (hunger, spells, rent). The engine must apply everything elapsed time does and the narrator must account for it. Clocks that only ever move to the maximum also create attractors: one clock that runs away drags everything that meets it, the behaviour a database paper shows drifting without bound when one number does two jobs ([Kulkarni et al.](https://cse.buffalo.edu/tech-reports/2014-04.pdf)). An innkeeper visited by the player ahead pulls every later visitor to that time, and a slow player who keeps being pulled never lives their own days.

The seventh gap is bookkeeping. Events at the same game minute need a fixed tie-break or recordings will not replay. Durations stored as absolute times on one global clock break when that clock is repointed, which a popular tabletop calendar tool warns about in its own documentation ([Simple Calendar docs](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/foundryvtt-simple-calendar@2.4.18/simple-calendar-docs/docs/using-sc/switching-calendars.md)). Doc 06's plan to have `ws.minutes` read "the acting character's clock" is the swap-in technique behind the RimWorld bug described above: any code that writes a global while one actor's time is swapped in is silently wrong.

The eighth gap is that long actions are all or nothing. The simulation standard used for linking simulators has a request that says "advance me to this time, or to the next thing that reaches me, whichever is first" ([Fujimoto, HLA slides](https://sigsim.acm.org/conf/mskr/Courseware/Fujimoto/Slides/FujimotoSlides-21-HLATimeManagement.pdf)). A rest or a journey should be that kind of request, so that an arrival can cut it short.

One further limit cannot be repaired by any design: friends talk outside the game, and no clock scheme sees it. The notes recall, without having re-read the passage, that Lamport's paper names this problem and that only keeping clocks close together limits the damage.

### One formula recurs under three names

The cautious school's central number is how far ahead a participant can promise not to affect anyone else, and its teaching example is an airport simulation where that promise is the shortest flight time between airports ([Fujimoto, null message slides](https://sigsim.acm.org/conf/mskr/Courseware/Fujimoto/Slides/FujimotoSlides-06-NullMessages.pdf)). Virtual-reality research reached the same place from the other side: each user may see distant users at a time offset, the offset must shrink to nothing as they approach, and that requires a speed limit on anything that carries influence ([Delaney et al. 2006](https://perso.lisn.upsaclay.fr/mbl/ENS/CSCW/2022/papers/Delaney2006.pdf)). In this game the speed limit is the road: **the time difference two characters can safely hold is the time it takes anything to travel between them**. If that holds, a journey toward someone always absorbs the gap and nobody is ever moved. A fixed window is a constant standing in for that function. This identification is the notes' reasoning; no source applies it to a game, and the virtual-reality result is known here through a 2006 survey because the original papers could not be retrieved.

## Four candidate designs, from repaired stamps to place clocks

All four candidates respect the owner's fixed decisions, and all four reduce to today's behaviour with one player, because one character's clock then equals the place's clock on every arrival. They share a groundwork that is worth building whichever is chosen. All times live on one axis of game minutes, and every read of "now" names whose clock it means; there is no global present. Events are ordered by game time, then by the host's sequence number, then by actor. A single routine, called carry forward here, moves a character to a later time, applies everything elapsed time does (healing, needs, rent, wages, effects running out), and produces a narrated account of how the time was spent; it never runs in the middle of a fight. An offline character is walked to the nearest safe place and checked out: absent from every scene, counted for nothing. Anything that leaves one place for another (news, a follow-on, a spreading disaster) lands at the later of its due time and the receiving place's clock, so it is never early and sometimes late. Model calls are prepared outside the transaction and committed only if what they read has not changed, otherwise ruled again. Long actions are requests that can be cut short. The examples use two players, Bryn and Kell, and Aldric the smith.

### Candidate A: stamps and a window (the current proposal, repaired)

| Rule | Statement |
|---|---|
| A1 Clocks and stamps | A clock per character; a last-used time on every person, thing and situation; a time per place. |
| A2 Touch, widened to reads | Before an action, find the latest of: the actor's clock, the place's time, and the stamp of everything the action will change or that will be put in front of the narrator. If that is later than the actor's clock, carry the actor forward to it first, asking when the gap exceeds the ask threshold (6 hours in doc 06) unless they are arriving. Afterward set all those stamps, and the place's time, to the actor's new clock. |
| A3 Perceive | A character is told only facts dated at or before their clock. The narrator is never given "nothing has happened" about a place whose time is behind the listener's clock. Effects from elsewhere land by the groundwork rule. |
| A4 Arriving ahead | A place's time may not pass the clock of a playing character standing in it. An arrival or a waking that would do so is held until those characters are out of any fight; they are then carried forward and asked how they spent the time, and the scene is sent. |
| A5 Window, scoped | A character may not advance more than the window past the slowest playing character within reach (in the same place, or able to arrive within the window by road). A long action that would cross it is cut short at a camp or inn and resumes when allowed. A returning character is carried to at least the slowest playing clock within reach. |

Engineering cost is the highest of the four. Stamps go on non-player characters, entities, threads and places, and every path that feeds the narrator (the speaker brief, the truth layer, news, town memory) must report what it read so the commit can stamp it. Soundness depends on that list being complete: a missed path is a silent contradiction that no test of the other paths will catch, the same class of fault as RimWorld's crossings. A prepared model call must be thrown away and prepared again (up to the 60 second budget, on that player's card) whenever a stamp it relied on moves before the commit. The window needs a reach calculation and rows for actions cut short. Lazy catch-up must learn to refuse to pass a resident. In the fiction, the player behind is moved without having chosen it, up to the window each time ("you lost two days because your friend reached the smith first"); arrival jumps are told as a slow road even when the road is an hour long; a walk can stop at the window's edge for no reason the story can give; and everything the player ahead touches pulls later visitors forward.

### Candidate B: the road-time rule (nobody is ever moved)

| Rule | Statement |
|---|---|
| B1 Clocks | A clock per character. No stamps. |
| B2 Horizon | A character may be at time t at position p only if, for every other playing character j, t is no later than j's clock plus the road time from j's position to p. Road time is the shortest time anything (a walker, a rider, a message) takes to cover the distance. |
| B3 Cut short and resume | An action that would cross the horizon is carried out as far as the horizon allows (a journey stops at the furthest camp; a rest gives what it has earned) and continues by itself as the horizon rises. The player may cancel and act at their current clock. |
| B4 Same place | Road time is zero, so characters in one place share one clock; the earliest acts, and group beats use the host's wait-or-window setting. |
| B5 Places and news | A place catches up lazily to the arriving clock, which rule B2 guarantees is never earlier than the place's time. News lands exactly when due. |
| B6 Offline | Checked out and removed from the horizon. On return, carried to the earliest time rule B2 allows. |

This is the cautious school's rule and the time track joined together. It needs clocks, a road-time lookup between any two positions (a shortest path on the existing road graph), a check before every action, and rows for cut-short actions that the host advances whenever another clock moves, each continuation being its own operation with a fresh narration prepared on the owner's card. There are no stamps and nothing is ever prepared twice. The risk is in play, not in code: every cost is real waiting on another human, the theory's "time creep", and any future feature that carries influence instantly (a sending spell, a teleport) drops road time to zero and locks the whole table together. In the fiction nothing strange ever happens, which no other candidate can claim; outside it, rests come in pieces, walks stall as friends draw near, and the slowest nearby player sets everyone's pace. It is the only candidate that is safe as it stands for players fighting each other.

### Candidate C: the dawn turn (one shared day)

| Rule | Statement |
|---|---|
| C1 Day and hour | One day number shared by all playing characters; each character keeps a time of day since dawn. |
| C2 Free hours | Within the day each character acts freely. An action that runs past the day's end, or a choice to turn in, marks the character done for the day. |
| C3 Dawn | Dawn fires when every playing character is done for the day; silence follows the host's window, with "turn in" as the default. In one transaction the day advances, every occupied place steps one day, effects from elsewhere land, rent and upkeep are billed, and multi-day actions advance by a day. |
| C4 Long actions | A journey or task of several days takes one dawn per day and can be cancelled at any dawn. |
| C5 Within a place | Characters in one place share its hour; whoever is behind is carried to it at their next moment outside a fight, by less than a day and without being asked. |
| C6 Offline | Checked out and not counted toward dawn; returns at the current day. |

This is the survival-game and turn-game answer, and the smallest change to the engine: a day counter close to today's campaign clock, an hour per character, and a dawn commit that touches every occupied place in one all-or-nothing transaction containing no model call, with each player's morning prepared afterward. Lazy regions are unchanged except that they catch up in whole days. Its weakness is the best documented complaint in the research: every dawn waits on the last player still up, and the wait grows with the number of players. Because time here moves only by actions, a player in minute-by-minute play can take an hour of real time to finish a day while a traveller looks at a blank screen. In the fiction it is the tidiest candidate (everyone shares the date, reunions are trivial, news arrives with the morning), at the price of pressure on the active player to end the day and a two-day walk that is two separate waits.

### Candidate D: place clocks with agreed skips (the novel hybrid)

| Rule | Statement |
|---|---|
| D1 The place keeps the clock | Each place (the unit the world simulation catches up lazily: a settlement, a stretch of road, a dungeon) has one clock. A character's own clock records the time they have lived and is used for billing. A fight is a closed scene inside its place with its own short count. Nobody is carried in the middle of one; what a fight changes outside itself lands on the place's clock when the fight ends; and an arrival at a fight fires when the fight's count reaches the arrival time or the fight ends, whichever is first. |
| D2 Short actions drift | An action shorter than the ask threshold happens at once and moves the actor's clock. The place's clock is the furthest clock among playing characters present. Anyone further behind it than the drift allowance is carried to within the allowance at their next action outside a fight, with a narrated line and no question. |
| D3 Long actions are offered | An action at or over the ask threshold (a night's rest, days of craft), ending at time T, happens at once if no other playing character is in the place. Otherwise the actor is busy until T, and each other playing character present is offered, at their next moment outside a fight, to pass the time too (choosing rest, work, training and so on) or to play on. When everyone present is busy or has agreed, the place's clock moves to the earliest end time among them and all are carried. Someone who plays on keeps the place at their pace; the actor may stop at any moment and keep the part already earned. Silence follows the host's wait-or-window setting. |
| D4 Travel brings clocks level, forward only | Arrival time is the later of the departure clock plus road time, and the destination's clock. If the destination is further ahead than the road accounts for, a gap under the ask threshold is told as delay on the road; a larger one is put to the traveller before leaving and spent as chosen downtime at the origin first. If the destination is behind and empty of players, it catches up lazily and the traveller arrives. If it is behind and occupied, the host commits and sends only the leaving; the arrival is pending. Those present are offered the skip to the arrival time under rule D3, and their own long actions are cut short by the arrival. Until then the traveller may wait, or break the journey and play on from the point of the road reached at the destination's current clock. |
| D5 Effects land late, never early | The groundwork landing rule is the whole of "perceive": a character knows what has landed in their place by its clock. |
| D6 Warn, do not block | Before a long action or journey that would leave a character more than the warn distance ahead of the slowest playing character within reach, the engine states the cost and asks. The host may set this to a wall (candidate A's window) or to the road-time rule (candidate B). |

The design takes one evidenced piece from each strong model: the clock per place from RimWorld and Larian, forward-only meeting from the Kerbal mods, paying for a gap in advance from the per-character-clock campaign, the vote scoped to the place from Minecraft, the deadline and default from Diplomacy and play by post, the interruptible request from the simulation standard, and showing a time cost before the player commits from Subterfuge, whose designers let players preview the outcome of an order before giving it ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/quality-of-life-in-subterfuge)). The combination is new and is this report's inference. Its organising idea is that the unit of shared time should be the unit of lazy simulation: the engine holds one version of a town, so a town can only be at one time, and every gap in candidate A that involved a place (reads, claims of absence, one town at two times) closes by construction instead of by stamping. Doc 07's rule that gains made behind the leader are held until the next sync also becomes unnecessary, because a hand-over needs both characters in one place and therefore at one clock.

Engineering cost is moderate. The proposal's region time becomes the authority; characters keep clocks; new rows hold busy-until times, pending arrivals and open offers. There are no stamps on people or things and no record of what the narrator read. Nothing speculative is ever prepared: for a journey toward slower players only the leaving goes to the model, and the arrival is prepared after the residents have answered. A carry in a place makes any turn being prepared there stale, to be ruled again, which is the main performance risk in a busy town. A skip's transaction waits for each carried player's passage, prepared in parallel on their own cards; a card that fails falls back to the narrator's stand-in line that already exists. In the fiction the costs are an occasional out-of-story question ("pass the night?") that hints someone else in the place is asleep or on the road; word of a far event sometimes arriving later than the road alone explains; time in a shared town running at the pace of its busiest player, so a short timed effect can run out sooner than its owner's own actions suggest; and a traveller heading toward friends who may sit on "you set out" for a few minutes.

The recommendation is candidate D, for four reasons. First, it is sound at the grain the engine already works at. The engine holds one version of each place and catches it up lazily, so "a place has one time" is a fact about the save file before it is a design choice; candidate A's stamps are an attempt to track finer distinctions that the stored world cannot represent, and its safety depends on never missing a read path. Second, every cost in D is visible to the player who bears it and, above a small threshold, chosen by them. The research is thin on how players react to being moved in time, but it is consistent that the worst-received rules are the ones that say "you may not play" and the ones that act on a character without its player; and the one well-told story about framing shows the same penalty accepted once it was presented as a benefit for time away (a secondhand account of the origin of World of Warcraft's rested bonus) ([The Psychology of Video Games](https://www.psychologyofgames.com/2010/03/framing-and-world-of-warcrafts-rest-system/)). An offered skip that pays out rest, wages or training is that framing. Third, nothing in D is a minimum or a vote over all players, so "no cap on players" costs nothing; a crowded town simply runs at the pace of its busiest occupant. Fourth, the other candidates are settings of D rather than rivals to it. Setting the answer to an offer to "always yes" gives candidate A's behaviour at the grain of a place; setting rule D6 to the road-time rule gives candidate B for exactly the players who need it, which is the shape of Civilization V's hybrid mode, where players at peace move together and players at war take turns (forum posts only) ([CivFanatics](https://forums.civfanatics.com/threads/hybrid-mode.532954)). Players fighting each other is therefore not ruled out, only not built.

The owner's original question, the size of the window, largely dissolves under this design. A wall two days wide becomes a warning, and the choice that matters moves to who gives way when clocks meet. If the owner prefers candidate A after all, the research supports three changes to the window regardless of its size: scope it to characters within reach of each other instead of the whole table, never let a returning player sit below it, and treat its width as an experience budget ("the longest skip the game may ask of anyone") to be tuned in play, because no theory picks the number. A width shorter than the longest routine journey will cut that journey in two whenever the traveller is already slightly ahead; that is an inference from the rule, not a finding.

### What to build first, what to leave as settings, and which numbers need a decision

The groundwork comes first, because it is identical under all four candidates and changes nothing for one player: the single time axis with no global present, the fixed ordering, the carry-forward routine, check-out and check-in for offline characters, and the landing rule for effects between places. Doc 06's migration steps 1, 3 and 4 (a clock per character, the perceive filter, catch-up per region) survive almost unchanged; step 2 (a last-touched time on every write) is not needed under D. The travel rule and the shared clock inside a place come second, testable with two characters on one machine as doc 06 planned, starting with destinations empty of players. Offers for long actions and pending arrivals come third, reusing the group-beat window. The warning and the drift rule come last, since they refine behaviour that is already safe.

Four things should be host settings: what silence means when an offer is open (wait, agree after the window, or play on), the length of that window, whether offers are asked or always taken as yes, and whether rule D6 warns, blocks or applies the road-time rule. Four numbers need the owner's choice, and the research supplies a basis for none of them. The ask threshold, the longest carry that happens without a yes, can stay at doc 06's 6 hours. The drift allowance, how far behind a place's clock a character may sit before being carried, should be near the smallest span the narration distinguishes, about an hour. The warn distance can inherit the proposal's two days, now harmless because it only prompts a question. The cap on downtime credited to a returning character has precedents only in other genres, where rest bonuses and offline progress are always capped ([Wurmpedia](https://wurmpedia.com/index.php/Sleep_bonus)).

The recommendation rests on inference in these places. No shipped game combines place clocks, consented skips and narration that cannot be recalled, so D has been tested against the five hard cases on paper only. No evidence was found on whether players prefer being asked to being moved, or on how long a player will wait for a friend's fight to end when the feed that would show the fight is off. The claim that late news reads naturally is a judgment about fiction, not a finding. And the notes on player-per-card generation found no precedent at all, so the cost of redone turns in a busy town is unmeasured until the first two-player night.

## Twelve questions the owner must settle first

| Question | Why it matters | Suggested answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1. When someone arrives ahead of a player already in a place, who gives way: the resident is asked, the resident is always carried, or the arriver waits? | This is the fairness choice the window was hiding. | Ask the resident; silence follows the host setting. |
| 2. May the engine ask an out-of-story question that hints at another player ("pass the night?", "pass two days?")? | It is not the meanwhile feed, but it leaks that someone nearby is asleep or on the road. | Yes, worded to reveal no deeds. |

| 5. What is the longest carry that may happen without a yes? | Sets how often players are asked. | Keep doc 06's 6 hours. |
| 6. Should the limit on running ahead be a warning, a wall, or the road-time rule, and how large? | A wall trades reunion gaps for blocked actions. | A warning at two days; wall and road-time kept as host choices. |
| 7. Is it acceptable that word of a far event sometimes reaches a place later than the road alone explains? | It is the price of never contradicting what a place has already been told. | Yes. |
| 8. What is a place: a whole settlement, a district, a dungeon, a leg of road? | A place has one clock, so its size sets how often players share one. | Whatever unit the world simulation catches up today; fights as closed scenes inside it. |
| 9. For a returning offline character: what is the default account of the gap, what is the cap on credited downtime, and are they brought level with friends within reach or only offered it? | Decides whether absence is neutral, rewarded or penalised. | "Rested; all is as it was"; a cap of the owner's choosing; offered, not forced. |
| 10. On which clock do world-wide situations (a cataclysm, a campaign-tier situation with a time limit) run? | They are the shared objects that broke in RimWorld and were removed in the Kerbal mods. | The slowest playing clock, landing in each place by the landing rule. |

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the question more than it answers it. The owner was asked for a number, and the literature's clearest statement about that number is that no one knows how to choose it. What the theory does settle is that a narrated game has exactly two tools, waiting and dating, and that every design is a policy for which player pays with which. Seen that way, the survey's long list of complaints sorts cleanly: locked characters and blocked nights are waiting imposed without consent, popping ghosts and unexpected jumps are dating imposed without consent, and the rules that lasted are the ones that scoped the decision to the people it touched and gave silence a default.

The second shift is about where the clock lives. The per-character clock is the natural idea and the one tabletop tradition hands down, but this engine does not store a world per character; it stores one town, caught up when someone arrives. Putting the clock where the single stored version already is removes most of the proposal's open cases without adding a rule, and leaves only one hard thing to build well: the moment a traveller walks into a place where someone else's present is still being played.
