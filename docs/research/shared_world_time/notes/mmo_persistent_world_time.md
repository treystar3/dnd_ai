# How massively multiplayer and persistent online worlds handle game time, travel, long activities and absent players

Reading notes for the report writer:

- Each key question below follows Takeaway / Cited Findings / Inferences / Gaps. Inside Cited Findings there is one sub-heading per game or model. The "fit" judgments for a small host-authoritative text co-op game are in Inferences, because they are mine, not the sources'.
- Source quality is marked where it matters: "official" (publisher page), "wiki" (community wiki), "forum" (player posts only), "snippet" (I saw only a search-result extract and did not open the page).
- Several pages could not be opened (HTTP 403/404/402): UOGuide, EVE University "Logging off", the Elite Dangerous fandom wiki, UESP, the Arelith wiki, Massively Overpowered articles, Bartle's "newbie" page on mud.co.uk. Claims that depended on them are in Gaps.
- Direct quotation is kept to a minimum; almost everything is paraphrase.

## 1. First-generation worlds: Ultima Online, EverQuest, Asheron's Call, Star Wars Galaxies, A Tale in the Desert, Dark Age of Camelot

### Takeaway
The first generation ran one shared real-time clock per server and made everything (travel, recovery, crafting upkeep) cost real minutes; an absent character was simply removed, with a short "linger" penalty for logging out somewhere unsafe, while property stayed in the world and decayed. Their distinctive contributions are the safe-logout place (UO inns), downtime defended as social time (EQ, SWG), a single story advanced monthly for everyone with per-server divergence (Asheron's Call), and a whole-world progression that ends and wipes (A Tale in the Desert).

### Cited Findings

#### Ultima Online (1997)
- Logout: a character is removed immediately only when logged out in a safe place and not combat-flagged. Safe places are city inns and taverns, the player's own house, a house where the player is friend or co-owner, and a temporary camping spot. Anywhere else the character stays in the world for five minutes and can be attacked. During those five minutes no other character on the account can log in to that shard. — [UO official: Concluding a Play Session](https://uo.com/?p=10697)
- Combat flag: after hitting or being hit, the player should wait about two minutes; otherwise the character lingers the full five minutes even in a safe place. — [UO official: Concluding a Play Session](https://uo.com/?p=10697); the same two-minute and five-minute figures appear on [UOGuide: Log Out](https://uoguide.com/Log_Out) (snippet)
- Camping: the Camping skill plus kindling and a bedroll gives a wilderness safe logout, but the wiki notes the skill is almost never used because even new players log out at an inn or tavern. — [UOGuide: Camping](https://www.uoguide.com/Camping) (snippet); [Ultima Codex: Camping](https://www.wiki.ultimacodex.com/wiki/Camping) (snippet)
- Free ("Endless Journey") accounts cannot use housing for safe logout and must use a city inn or tavern. — [UO official: Endless Journey](https://uo.com/endless-journey/) (snippet)
- House decay: property persists while the owner is away but must be refreshed. Sources disagree on timings because the rules changed by publish and differ on player-run shards: one free-shard wiki gives roughly four weeks from refreshed to collapsed with a final "In Danger of Collapsing" stage of about six hours; a 2002 official publish note describes condemned houses that cannot be refreshed decaying within five days; Publish 108 (2020) defines six decay stages over at least five days with a collapse timer shown on the sign; UOGuide says three to five days with the fall 5 to 15 hours into the last stage. — [UO Forever wiki (player-run shard): House Decay](https://uoforever.com/wiki/index.php/House_Decay); [UO official: Publish 16](https://uo.com/?p=11280); [UO official: Publish 108](https://uo.com/?p=21914); [UOGuide: IDOC](https://www.uoguide.com/IDOC) (all snippets)
- Refreshing is done by the owner, co-owner or a friend touching the sign, a teleporter tile or a door. — [UO Forever wiki: House Decay](https://uoforever.com/wiki/index.php/House_Decay) (snippet, player-run shard)

#### EverQuest (1999)
- Clock: 6 real seconds is one server tick; 1 real minute is 20 game minutes, so one game hour is 3 real minutes and a full game day is 72 real minutes. The clock is shared by everyone on the server; time of day drives NPC pathing and spawns. — [Project 1999 wiki: Time](https://wiki.project1999.com/Time) (snippet; Project 1999 is a classic-era emulator, so this documents the 1999 to 2001 rules)
- Downtime numbers (one blogger's estimates, 2006): a level 42 druid needed about 15 minutes of meditation to refill mana after a 5-minute pull (about 10 with an enchanter's Clarity buff); boats could mean up to a 20-minute wait; WoW by contrast refilled a priest's mana in about 3 minutes standing or under a minute drinking, and ran boats and zeppelins every 5 minutes. The same post puts an average first character at about 2,000 hours to level 60 in EQ versus about 500 in WoW (a commenter disputed the EQ figure). — [Tobold's blog, "Changes part 3: time" (2006)](https://tobolds.blogspot.com/2006/06/changes-part-3-time.html?m=1)
- The developers' stated defense of downtime was that it gave groups time to talk and so built friendships; the blogger suspects it also stretched content, and concludes that many players hated it and that WoW's faster pace won far more customers. — [Tobold's blog (2006)](https://tobolds.blogspot.com/2006/06/changes-part-3-time.html?m=1)
- A 2026 article on a new EverQuest classic-style server quotes its makers saying a meaningful original-EQ session took six to eight hours, and that the audience no longer has that time. — [GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/mmo/everquest-is-the-next-legacy-mmo-to-get-the-classic-treatment-after-wow-classic-and-old-school-runescape-but-with-a-genius-twist-it-knows-all-of-us-olds-dont-have-time-for-mmos-anymore/) (snippet)

#### Asheron's Call (1999 to 2017)
- The monthly updates composed one continuing story in episodes and arcs, with occasional live world events; launched 2 November 1999, last content patch 4 March 2014, servers closed 31 January 2017. — [Wikipedia: Asheron's Call](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asheron%27s_Call)
- Shard of the Herald (November 2000, "Should the Stars Fall"): the last crystal imprisoning the villain Bael'Zharon could be attacked by players who sided with him. On most servers it was destroyed quickly. On the Thistledown server players organized shift "vigils" and defended it for much of each day, and exploited mechanics to strengthen it, until developers stepped in with controlled characters and destroyed it so the story could proceed. — [Loresraat wiki: 2000/11 Shard of the Herald](https://loresraat.org/wiki/2000/11_-_Shard_of_the_Herald); developer-controlled generals also stated on [Drunkenfell wiki](https://www.drunkenfell.com/index.php?title=TD) (snippet). Both are fan wikis.
- Permanent mark: a monument listing the defenders' names stands outside Ithaenc Cathedral on Thistledown only; the next month's event was "Bael'Zharon's Rampage". — [Loresraat wiki](https://loresraat.org/wiki/2000/11_-_Shard_of_the_Herald)

#### Star Wars Galaxies (2003)
- Wounds could only be healed at hospitals and at camps built by Rangers; "battle fatigue" was a slower accumulating debility that medicine could not fix and that only watching entertainers (dancers, musicians) in a cantina removed. The intended loop was buff, fight, heal at camp, unwind at the bar. Koster says the loop never worked properly because the team could not tell how long a session was, and admits the visit was simply forced. — [Raph Koster, "Designing a Living Society in SWG, part two" (2015)](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=25743)
- Koster's own later diagnosis: forced downtime was accepted at natural stopping points, but battle fatigue was mistimed. It built up too fast and did not clear while the player was logged out. — [Raph Koster, "Forcing interaction" (9 December 2005)](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=198)
- A retrospective describes fatigue as negligible at first but crippling if left untreated, with the cantina the only cure. — [Kill Screen](https://killscreen.com/articles/remembering-the-beautifully-boring-mmo-star-wars-galaxies/) (snippet)
- Shuttles: players waited at shuttleports for scheduled pickups and used the wait to chat or craft; a 2009 fan blog remembers this fondly but argues it should not return. A five-minute interval between pickups at launch appears in a search extract whose page I did not open. — [SWG fan blog (2009)](https://swgvincer.blogspot.com/2009/02/classic-swg-waiting-for-shuttle.html) (snippet); [Engadget SWG history (2008)](https://www.engadget.com/2008-06-26-a-star-wars-galaxies-history-lesson-from-launch-to-the-nge-2.html) (snippet, attribution of the five-minute figure uncertain)
- Offline production: harvesters extract resources unattended as long as maintenance and power are paid; unpaid, they stop, decay and eventually vanish; maintenance is drawn automatically but power needs a visit. — [Archived SWG manual/FAQ at SWGEmu](https://www.swgemu.com/archive/scrapbookv51/data/20070128130746/manual_detail_094.html); [SWG Restoration wiki: Droid Engineer](https://swgr.org/wiki/droid_engineer) (snippets, fan-archived)
- Koster notes harvesters, pets and droids were meant to tie players to the world through ownership, and that player cities were abused (for example to block dungeon entrances). — [Raph Koster (2015)](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=25743)
- Henry Jenkins records that Koster's original policies were eroded over time, raising player frustration and distrust. — [Henry Jenkins blog (2006)](https://www.henryjenkins.org/blog/2006/07/so_what_happened_to_star_wars.html) (snippet)

#### A Tale in the Desert (2003 onward)
- A "Telling" is one complete run of the game: the game actually ends, the world and characters are wiped, and everyone restarts, with rule changes between Tellings. Telling 1 ran 15 February 2003 to 2 September 2004; Telling 2 to 24 May 2006; Telling 3 to 11 December 2008; Telling 8 ran 2 March 2018 to 30 August 2019; Telling 9 to 25 May 2021; Telling 10 began 29 May 2021. — [Wikipedia: A Tale in the Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Tale_in_the_Desert) (the article is flagged for weak citations)
- The shared goal is for the whole population to pass Tests in seven disciplines so seven Monuments are built before the Telling ends; only Telling 1 is described as "won". Players can pass laws by petition that change rules within what developers can code. An "Offline Flax" trait lets a character gather while logged out. — [Wikipedia: A Tale in the Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Tale_in_the_Desert)

#### Dark Age of Camelot (2001)
- "Camp bonus": experience at a hunting spot falls as the spot is overhunted and recovers over time; a 2004 patch slowed the decay and lengthened recovery for badly overhunted camps; underpopulated realms got a larger bonus. — [GameBanshee: Camelot patch notes](https://gamebanshee.com/k6943); [GameBanshee: population bonus changes](https://www.gamebanshee.com/qufa) (snippets)
- A realm timer of five minutes stops a player switching between realms on the same cluster (2010 patch note). — [Camelot Herald wiki patch notes](https://camelotherald.fandom.com/wiki/Patch_Notes:_Version_1.107) (snippet, attribution to this exact version uncertain)

### Inferences
- Fit of the UO model: the inn rule transfers almost unchanged. "Log out at an inn and you are safely gone; log out in the wild and your character is exposed for a while" is a rule about where an absent character is, which a small persistent world needs an answer to. The five-minute figure is an anti-combat-logging device and matters only where other players can hurt you; in co-op the transferable part is the place rule, not the timer.
- House decay is a scale mechanism (land scarcity among thousands). For a few friends it only punishes absence.
- Fit of the EQ model: the fixed clock ratio (72 real minutes per day) works because every action takes real time. It cannot express "this action took three days"; a text game where one command spans days needs time to be advanced by actions, not by the wall clock. EQ's real lesson is the reception: enforced waiting was defended as social glue and was then outcompeted by a game that cut it by a factor of three to five.
- Asheron's Call is the closest precedent for "one story, advanced for everyone, that players can bend": each server's history really differed (Thistledown held the shard for weeks) and the divergence was honored with a permanent object in that server's world, while the authors still forced the plot back onto its track. For a small group this is the cheap, proven answer to one-time events: let the outcome differ, and leave a durable in-world record that absent players can read.
- SWG's battle fatigue is a warning about tying recovery to a place and a real-time wait: its own designer says the fault was pacing and that time offline did not count. A recovery cost paid in game time that the engine advances (rather than in the player's real minutes) avoids the complaint while keeping the fiction.
- SWG harvesters are the earliest clear case of "declare it, pay upkeep, it runs while you are gone". This transfers well: a long activity becomes a job with a completion time on the shared clock.
- A Tale in the Desert shows whole-world progression can be the game, at the cost of an ending and a wipe. For a persistent campaign the transferable idea is the shared milestone that everyone advances, not the reset.
- DAoC's camp bonus is a lazily recovering per-place counter (world state that heals with elapsed time), which is cheap to compute on demand in a host-authoritative engine.

## 2. EVE Online beyond time dilation

### Takeaway
EVE treats real time as the game clock for everything long: skills train and industry jobs run to a completion timestamp whether or not the player is online, and an absent player's assets are protected by timers the owner sets in advance to their own time zone. Logging out removes the ship only after timers that depend on recent aggression.

### Cited Findings
- Skill training runs in real time and continues while logged off; the standard advice is to always have a skill training. A default paying ("Omega") character gains about 30 skill points a minute, a free ("Alpha") one about 15. Level V of a rank-1 skill needs 256,000 points (about 5.9 days at the default rate, by arithmetic from the wiki's figures); a rank-16 skill needs 4,096,000; one rank-4 skill takes nearly 25 days from IV to V. — [EVE University wiki: Skills and learning](https://wiki.eveuniversity.org/Skills_and_learning)
- Industry: jobs show a duration and an end time in the Jobs tab, and the player must come back and "deliver" finished jobs; that is, a finished job waits. — [EVE University wiki: Industry](https://wiki.eveuniversity.org/Industry) (snippet)
- Logging off in space: a "non-capsuleer" log-off timer (shooting or being shot by NPCs) lasts 5 minutes; a "capsuleer" log-off timer (player combat) lasts 15 minutes and is renewed by each hostile act; with player aggression the ship stays in space until 15 minutes after logoff or after the last hostile act against it. — [EVE University wiki: Timers](https://wiki.eveuniversity.org/Timers) (snippet); [EVE forums archive](https://forums-archive.eveonline.com/topic/176208/5) (forum, 2012)
- Safe logoff (added with the Retribution expansion, 2012): the ship sits for 30 seconds with modules off and then is removed; it cannot be started while timers are running. An ordinary disconnect without aggression makes the ship warp away and disappear after about a minute. — [PC Gamer on Retribution](https://www.pcgamer.com/eve-online-retribution-logout-system-no-crime-toggle) (snippet); [EVE forums archive, CCP post](https://forums-archive.eveonline.com/topic/176208/5) (forum, 2012)
- Complaint: a 2020-era forum thread argues the 15-minute capsuleer timer is too long for casual play. — [EVE forums](https://forums.eveonline.com/t/15-minutes-capsuleer-log-off-timer-is-too-long-for-casual-play/242608) (forum; title only seen)
- Jump fatigue (Phoebe release, late 2014): a per-character penalty that grows with distance jumped and limits how soon the next jump is allowed, meant to make jump travel rare and local; it never applies to stargate travel. Players read the aim as stopping coalitions from moving capital fleets across the whole map in minutes. Commentators noted jump clones and fast ships as workarounds. — [Engadget (30 October 2014)](https://www.engadget.com/2014-10-30-eve-online-uses-jump-fatigue-to-discourage-long-distance-travel.html); [CCP dev blog: Phoebe travel change update](https://www.eveonline.com/article/phoebe-travel-change-update) (snippets)
- Structure reinforcement (Upwell 2.0, 13 February 2018): the owner chooses an hour of the day and a day of the week at which a reinforced structure's later timers will come out; changes to that choice take 7 days to apply; the default was Saturday 18:00 EVE time. — [CCP: Upwell 2.0 structure changes](https://www.eveonline.com/ko/news/view/upwell-2.0-structures-changes-coming-on-february-13th); [CCP: action required by structure operators](https://www.eveonline.com/article/p41u8d) (snippets, official)
- A player thread says the actual exit time is randomized within a window of some hours around the chosen hour (forum only, unconfirmed). — [EVE forums: Reinforcement Timer Logic](https://forums.eveonline.com/t/reinforcement-timer-logic/475932)
- One-time live events: a 2009 column reports that EVE's early developer-run and volunteer-run events happened in one star system at one time, drew fewer than a hundred participants from a game of over 300,000 players, strained the server when announced, and that the volunteer event team was closed after leaks. It recommends events lasting several days to a week in several places at once. — [Engadget, "EVE Evolved: World events in EVE Online" (7 June 2009)](https://www.engadget.com/2009-06-07-eve-evolved-world-events-in-eve-online.html)

### Inferences
- The skill queue and industry job are the cleanest existing form of "a long activity as a job with an end time". They transfer directly if "real time" is replaced by the game clock: a character who starts ten days of training is busy until day N, and any other character's action that advances the shared clock past N completes it. EVE never has to resolve two players being at different times, because nobody can skip time; a text game where one action spans days does have to, so the job model needs a rule for who is allowed to advance the clock.
- "Finished jobs wait for delivery" is a useful contradiction guard: completion is computed from the timestamp, but its effects enter the world only when the owner is present to collect, so nothing surprising happens to an absent player's inventory.
- Reinforcement timers solve asynchronous conflict: an attacker cannot finish off an absent owner's property except at a time the owner chose. In a co-op world the equivalent principle is that the world should not irreversibly resolve a threat to an absent player's stake without a window in which that player can act.
- Jump fatigue and the aggression log-off timers solve problems of thousands of adversaries (force projection, combat logging). They do not transfer.
- The world-events critique transfers in miniature: an event bound to one place and one moment is seen by whoever happens to be there. With a handful of players the remedy is not longer events but a reliable account for those who were elsewhere.

### Gaps
- EVE University "Logging off" page returned 404, and no current official support article on safe logoff or structure timers was retrieved; the logoff figures above are from a community wiki snippet, a 2012 forum post and a 2012 press article, so current values may differ.
- Daily server downtime (I recall a fixed daily restart at 11:00 UTC) was not sourced.
- Whether the industry job clock, skill queue length limits, or planetary industry extraction cycles have changed recently: not checked.

## 3. Instancing, megaservers and phasing: Guild Wars 1 and 2, Final Fantasy XIV, The Elder Scrolls Online, Destiny, The Division, Fallout 76, No Man's Sky, Elite Dangerous, Star Citizen (and what is new in World of Warcraft)

### Takeaway
After 2004 the industry stopped trying to show everyone one world. It splits the population into many copies (instances, megaserver channels, layers), gives each player a private version of story state (phases, per-player loot and quest state, solo story instances), and keeps the shared space free of permanent consequences. The recurring, well-documented cost is that friends at different story stages cannot see each other or cannot share progress, and that one-time story content strands latecomers.

### Cited Findings

#### Guild Wars 1 (2005)
- Explorable areas are instanced: only the party that created the instance can enter it. Towns and outposts are the shared, non-instanced places where players group, trade and socialize, and they hold the exits to explorable areas. — [Guild Wars wiki: Explorable area](https://wiki.guildwars.com/wiki/Explorable_area); [Guild Wars wiki: Outpost](https://wiki-en.guildwars.com/wiki/Outpost) (snippets)
- The community wiki gives the reason as removing griefing such as preying on weaker players and kill stealing. No official ArenaNet statement was found. — [GuildWiki: Instance](https://guildwiki.gamepedia.com/Instance) (snippet, community-written)

#### Guild Wars 2 (2012)
- Megaserver (rollout completed 21 April 2014) replaced per-world map copies and overflow maps: on entering a map a player is placed in a copy chosen by heuristics that weigh party, guild, language and home world; regions (North America, Europe, China) stay separate. A party member can be followed with "Join in <zone>"; since 18 April 2023 there is a queue when the friend's copy is full. Players cannot pick a copy directly. — [Guild Wars 2 wiki: Megaserver](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Megaserver)
- Dynamic events start on conditions and proceed whether or not anyone takes part; outcomes trigger follow-up events (raiders who succeed occupy a fort, which can then be retaken), so a map's state moves forward and back. The wiki states plainly that because events repeat their consequences are ultimately temporary, though a state can last days. Events scale with the number of players present, mostly up to about ten. The stated aims were to replace quests, favor cooperation and avoid griefing. — [Guild Wars 2 wiki: Dynamic event](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Dynamic_event)
- Living World Season 1 ran 28 January 2013 to 18 March 2014 in temporary installments roughly every two weeks; most of it then became unavailable for years. It was re-released free as five permanent episodes from 19 April 2022 to 8 November 2022 (one arc was left out). — [Guild Wars 2 wiki: Living World Season 1](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Living_World_Season_1); [Guild Wars 2 wiki: Living World](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/LW) (snippet)
- The wiki says the lack of permanence and repeatability led ArenaNet to change approach in summer 2014: new maps plus repeatable story instances replayed from a Story Journal. — [Guild Wars 2 wiki: Living World](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/LW) (snippet)
- The designer of the 2022 rebuild said Season 1 had not been designed to fit the Story Journal and had to be restructured, and that story-driven map changes were allowed to stay permanent where they added to the map. — [Game Rant interview with Joe Kimmes](https://gamerant.com/guild-wars-2-interview-living-world-season-1-modernization-challenges-joe-kimmes/) (snippet)
- Player complaint: as more players missed parts of the story, the developers kept having to add ways to catch up, and newcomers found the game harder to enter. — [Guild Wars 2 forum thread](https://en-forum.guildwars2.com/topic/62504-fortnites-events-is-what-gw2s-living-world-was-supposed-to-be) (forum, snippet)

#### Final Fantasy XIV (2013 relaunch)
- Story is delivered through instanced duties. Some quest battles must be done solo (usually with NPC companions). Duty Support (patch 6.1, extended through 6.5) lets a player run every main-story dungeon and four-player trial with NPCs, but it cannot be used while in a party with other players; eight-player story trials still need matchmaking. — [FFXIV community wiki: Duty Support](https://ffxiv.consolegameswiki.com/wiki/Duty_Support); [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/final-fantasy-14s-latest-patch-adds-npc-support-for-solo-players/) (snippets)

#### The Elder Scrolls Online (2014)
- One megaserver per platform and region instead of named servers; inside it each zone runs as several copies ("channels"), so a player does not see everyone in the zone. — [UESP: Megaservers](https://en.uesp.net/wiki/Online:Megaservers) (snippet; page itself returned 403)
- Phasing is a second layer: players at different stages of a quest stay in the same channel and share resources but stop seeing each other; not having started a quest usually counts as stage zero. The wiki calls this unavoidable until the group members reach the same stage. — [UESP: Megaservers](https://en.uesp.net/wiki/Online:Megaservers) (snippet)
- Complaints (forum only): grouped friends vanish, there is no indicator that a group member is in another phase, and the system was called anti-social for an online game; the workaround is "travel to player". — [ESO forum: friend in group disappearing](https://forums.elderscrollsonline.com/en/discussion/161407/friend-in-group-disappearing) and related threads (forum)
- One Tamriel (2016) scaled all zones so players of any level and alliance could group anywhere outside PvP; the creative director later credited it with much of the game's success. — [UESP: One Tamriel](https://en.m.uesp.net/wiki/Online:One_Tamriel); [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/eso-dev-one-tamriel-best-thing-we-did/) (snippets)

#### World of Warcraft: what is new beyond classic phasing
- Chromie Time (a per-character choice of which expansion's timeline the leveling world is in) and Zidormi (an NPC who switches a zone between its past and present state) put players in the same place into different time versions. Forum threads report friends and family unable to see or play with each other, Party Sync failing to align phases, and role-play in one zone being unusable when characters were not all at maximum level. — [Blizzard forum: stuck in Chromie Time](https://us.forums.blizzard.com/en/wow/t/i-cant-play-with-my-lower-level-family-members-stuck-in-chromie-time/1972491); [Blizzard EU forum: Dragon Isles unusable for RP](https://eu.forums.blizzard.com/en/wow/t/psa-dragon-isles-currently-unusable-for-rp-purposes-if-all-characters-are-not-max-level/525313); [Blizzard forum: Party Sync](https://us.forums.blizzard.com/en/wow/t/party-sync/1346553) (forum only)

#### Destiny and Destiny 2
- Architecture as described by Wikipedia (flagged citation needed): a world server supplies persistent data to on-demand instances called "bubbles". — [Wikipedia: Persistent world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_world)
- The Destiny Content Vault (2020) removed campaigns, destinations and raids because the game had become too large to maintain; critics said new players could no longer play major chapters of the story. Bungie later said expansions from Lightfall on would not be vaulted (seasonal content still leaves), and more recently that it has begun work on bringing vaulted content back. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/au/can-removing-half-of-destiny-2-really-be-good-for-the-game); [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/destiny-2-tossing-away-old-story-content-is-making-it-h-1848323271); [NME](https://www.nme.com/features/gaming-features/destiny-2-isnt-kind-to-new-players-3137127); [Inven Global](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/26337/bungie-to-bring-back-vaulted-destiny-2-content-as-it-resets-studio-priorities) (snippets)

#### The Division 1 and 2
- Story missions are scripted instances played solo, with friends or by matchmaking; the Dark Zone is a shared space entered without a loading screen and capped (fan wiki says 24 players per instance). In The Division 2, completing main missions upgrades the player's base and settlements, and each Dark Zone is introduced through a private instance before becoming shared. — [The Division wiki: Missions](https://thedivision.fandom.com/wiki/Missions_in_Tom_Clancy%27s_The_Division); [Ubisoft: Welcome to the Dark Zones](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-ca/game/the-division/news-updates/32cYt1IsEhUTxsVgIlDZKi/intelligence-annex-welcome-to-the-dark-zones); [Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/preview/2019/01/15/10-things-you-need-to-know-about-the-division-2s-new-dark-zones-and-conflict-pvp) (snippets)

#### Fallout 76 (2018; Wastelanders 2020)
- Loot containers are instanced per player on a shared server: Bethesda said arriving to find a container already emptied was unsatisfying. The looted state follows the account across servers for a time. — [GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/fallout-76-has-instanced-loot-modded-multiplayer-servers-discussed); [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/bethesda-clarifies-fallout-76s-private-server-issu/1100-6470873/) (snippets)
- Wastelanders put human NPCs and story choices in instanced interiors. Entering one separates the player from everyone not on the team; teammates may enter the leader's instance and listen to the conversation, but have no influence and gain no progress, or may enter their own copy instead. Choices and progression cannot be shared. — [Bethesda: Wastelanders preview panel recap](https://fallout.bethesda.net/en-NZ/news/fallout-76-inside-the-vault-wastelanders-preview-panel-recap-and-gameplay); [Wccftech review](https://wccftech.com/review/fallout-76-wastelanders-country-roads-take-me-home/); [TweakTown](https://www.tweaktown.com/news/70946/fallout-76-is-now-more-like-singleplayer-game-than-ever/index.html) (snippets, 2020 launch coverage)

#### No Man's Sky (2016; Expeditions 2021)
- Expeditions are seasonal, time-limited community modes in which every player starts on the same planet with shared goals; they are periodically rerun for a few days (the Omega expedition ran 15 to 19 February 2024). — [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/no-mans-sky-expeditions-update-adds-seasons-in-new-multiplayer-game-mode/1100-6489572/); [Gematsu](https://www.gematsu.com/2024/02/no-mans-sky-omega-update-now-available) (snippets)

#### Elite Dangerous (2014)
- The galaxy clock is real time: synchronized with UTC and set 1,286 years ahead (2014 is 3300). Planets orbit and rotate in real time. A background simulation changes systems, economies and governments from aggregated player actions; community goals and story arcs (two Thargoid wars, the 2017 player-run "Salome" event) leave lasting changes. Offline single-player was dropped in November 2014, to backer complaints. — [Wikipedia: Elite Dangerous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elite_Dangerous)
- Solo, Private Group and Open are filters on who you can meet; all feed the same background simulation on every platform. This is a long-running complaint: forum posters argue Solo and Private play should not be able to affect what Open players see; defenders say a single shared galaxy regardless of mode, time zone or platform is the point. — [Frontier forums](https://forums.frontier.co.uk/goto/post?id=8629083) and related threads (forum only)
- The simulation advances in a daily "tick"; Frontier has posted about making the tick time consistent and about fixes taking effect at the next tick. — [Frontier forums: thoughts on tick consistency](https://forums.frontier.co.uk/threads/elite-dangerous-background-simulations-addressing-thoughts-on-tick-consistency.614365/latest); [Gaming Nexus](https://www.gamingnexus.com/News/43299/Elite-Dangerous-update-to-combat-issues-that-keep-popping-up-in-Background-Simulation) (snippets)
- Meeting is peer-to-peer and instanced, with a commonly cited limit of 32 players per instance; players report friends failing to land in the same instance and trade recipes for forcing it (wing beacons, dropping one at a time). — [Frontier forums: how many players per instance](https://forums.frontier.co.uk/threads/how-many-people-can-be-in-the-same-system-instance-at-the-same-time.116994); [Fuel Rats procedures](https://confluence.fuelrats.com/x/TgBh) (forum and community, snippets)

#### Star Citizen (alpha)
- Logging out in a ship's bed persists the ship and character position; long-term persistence was first tested in Alpha 3.8.2, where a ship destroyed after bed logout returned the player to their origin. Static server meshing arrived in Alpha 4.0.0 so more players share one universe without separate servers. Bed logout remains a source of bugs (a 2026 report of docked ships vanishing). — [Star Citizen wiki: Alpha 4.0.0](https://starcitizen.tools/Update%3AStar_Citizen_Alpha_4.0.0); [RSI Spectrum: 3.8.2 patch notes](https://robertsspaceindustries.com/spectrum/community/SC/forum/190048/thread/star-citizen-alpha-3-8-2-ptu-4477985-patch-notes/2792264); [The Impound](https://theimpound.com/blogs/star-citizen-news/star-citizen-carrier-bed-logout-bug-docked-fighters-can-disappear) (snippets)

### Inferences
- Megaservers, channels, layers and the 32-player instance are answers to "too many people in one place" and "too few people in one place". A game for a few friends has neither problem; there is exactly one copy of the world and that is the feature. None of this transfers.
- Phasing and per-player story state are answers to "thousands of players must each be the one who saved the village". The price, documented for ESO, WoW and Fallout 76, is that people who came to play together are separated or cannot share the consequence. A small shared world should take the opposite choice: one world state, where the first party to resolve a matter resolves it for everyone, and the others learn of it. The Fallout 76 "listen in but cannot influence" rule is the specific thing to avoid.
- Per-player instanced loot (Fallout 76) is the same trade at small scale: it removes the disappointment of an emptied chest by making the chest not really shared. In a world meant to change permanently for everyone, an emptied chest is correct and should be narrated as such.
- Guild Wars 2's dynamic events are deliberately reversible so that the next visitor has something to do; the wiki itself says consequences are ultimately temporary. That is the reverse of "the world changes permanently in response to players" and is needed only because the audience is unbounded and anonymous. What transfers is the mechanism (events that proceed on their own whether or not players attend, with outcomes that set up the next event), not the reset.
- Living World Season 1 and the Destiny vault are the canonical "one-time event problem": content that happens once is fine for those present and a wall for those who arrive later. Both studios reversed course, and Guild Wars 2's fix was a journal from which past story can be replayed. For a small persistent world the event can stay truly one-time (that is the point of permanence), but there must be a durable in-world account so that a player who was away or elsewhere is not left with a hole. This matches the Asheron's Call monument.
- Guild Wars 1 is structurally the closest large game to a small co-op: shared hubs, private adventures per party. Its weakness was that two parties never affected the same world. The target game wants the hub and the private excursions to be the same persistent place.
- Elite Dangerous is the best large-scale precedent for "the world keeps moving on its own clock and everyone's actions, wherever and with whomever they were taken, are folded into one state at a tick". A host-authoritative engine can do the same thing more simply: world simulation advances in discrete steps keyed to the game clock, applied once, regardless of which sub-party caused the clock to move. The complaint about Solo affecting Open is a fairness complaint between strangers and does not arise among friends.
- Chromie Time and Zidormi show what happens when players in one place are allowed to be at different times: they cannot see each other, and the tools for re-synchronizing are fragile. This is direct evidence against letting split parties drift into separate time phases of the same location without a defined rule for how they meet again.
- FFXIV's solo story instances and NPC party members are a workaround for story beats that must not depend on other players being available; in a small co-op the analogous case is a player acting while the others are offline, where NPC companions (or the absent characters run by the engine) fill the same role.

### Gaps
- No Man's Sky: how base building, terrain edits and discoveries are shared between a host's world and visitors was not found; only Expeditions were covered.
- Elite Dangerous: no Frontier statement on how actions in each mode are weighted at the tick; mode-sharing rests on forum and fan-site statements. The fandom wiki page on the background simulation returned HTTP 402.
- ESO: no developer or conference source on megaserver placement rules for friends and guilds; UESP returned 403 and is represented by a search extract.
- The Division: nothing found on whether world state follows the group leader; coverage is thin.
- Fallout 76: nothing found on CAMP placement conflicts or what happens to workshops at logout. Sources are from 2018 to 2020 and may be out of date.
- Star Citizen: no clear current statement of logout rules, time of day or long-distance travel times; the game changes too often for the sources found.
- No conference talk (GDC or similar) on megaserver or instancing design was retrieved in this pass.
- Destiny's weekly reset timing and complaints about it were not found.

## 4. Real time as the game clock: Animal Crossing, daily and weekly resets, idle and offline-progress games

### Takeaway
These games bind the game clock to the wall clock, so players who play at different times are, by construction, at the same game time; absence is handled by simulating on return what "would have happened". The costs are that the clock can be forged, and that events tied to dates exclude people who were not there.

### Cited Findings
- Animal Crossing: the island follows the console clock; players "time travel" by changing it (in New Horizons by turning off internet clock synchronization). Consequences are simulated on return: weeds grow, turnips rot after any time travel, in older games villagers could move away. — [Nookipedia: Time travel](https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Time_travel)
- In New Horizons, before version 2.0 some seasonal events could be reached only on their real dates (they were delivered online); since 2.0 all events recur every year and can be reached by clock changes. Villagers now need the player's permission to leave. — [Nookipedia: Time travel](https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Time_travel)
- Animal Crossing is cited as an example of simulated persistence: it generates events that could have occurred while the player was away rather than running continuously. — [Wikipedia: Persistent world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_world)
- Dream visits: an island uploaded as a "dream" is frozen at the time of day and season of the upload, and visitors see that. — [Twinfinite guide (2020)](https://twinfinite.net/2020/07/animal-crossing-new-horizons-dream-suite-visit-other-players-islands/) (snippet)
- The developers have said they think the game is better in the long run without time travel. — [GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/au/animal-crossing-new-horizons-developers-think-playing-without-time-travels-best-in-the-long-run/) (snippet)
- Idle games: design writing treats the offline cap as a deliberate lever, there to stop unattended compounding progress from swallowing active play. Examples reported: Melvor Idle caps offline progress at 18 hours; Clicker Heroes has no 24-hour cap. Variants include a reduced offline rate, a stored "time away" resource the player spends to speed the game up, and staggered timers with different periods (minutes, hours, days) so players on different schedules each have something due. — [Clicker Heroes blog](https://blog.clickerheroes.com/?p=1902); [Game Developer: The Math of Idle Games, part III](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/the-math-of-idle-games-part-iii); [Machinations: how to design idle games](https://machinations.io/articles/idle-games-and-how-to-design-them); Melvor figure from [itemlevel.net](https://itemlevel.net/is-melvor-idle-online-only/) (all snippets; several of the search results on this topic were low-quality or promotional and were not used)

### Inferences
- The real-time-clock model avoids the split-party time problem entirely, but only because no action can consume more or less time than it takes to perform. It is incompatible with "one typed action can consume seconds or days". What transfers is the technique of lazy catch-up: on a player's return (or when the game clock jumps), compute what would have happened in the elapsed interval instead of having simulated it tick by tick. This is the same family as the lazy resets the requester already knows.
- The dream-visit rule (the visitor adopts the frozen time of the place visited) and ordinary island visits are examples of resolving a time difference by declaring one party's clock authoritative for the meeting. That is a usable rule for reuniting split parties: the meeting happens at the later of the two clocks, and the earlier party is deemed to have waited.
- Time travel by clock forging shows that when players can move the clock freely, they will use it to harvest time-gated rewards. In a co-op game where actions advance the clock, any benefit that accrues per unit of game time (rest, crops, interest) can be farmed by "waiting", so such benefits need caps or diminishing returns, as idle games learned.
- The stored "time away" resource from idle games is a close relative of rested experience and of Wurm's sleep bonus (see section 6).

### Gaps
- How an ordinary (non-dream) multiplayer island visit reconciles the host's and the visitor's dates was not found; the commonly stated rule (the visitor sees the host island's date and time) is unverified here.
- Pokemon daily events and Destiny weekly resets: no sources retrieved. Not reported.
- No strong primary design article on idle-game offline caps was opened; the findings rest on search extracts.

## 5. Persistent small-group worlds: Neverwinter Nights servers, text role-play (MUSH) time ratios, Minecraft, Valheim, Conan Exiles, ARK, Project Gorgon, Wurm Online, Haven and Hearth, RuneScape

### Takeaway
Small persistent servers face the target game's problems most directly. Their answers are: pause the world when nobody is on (Minecraft since 1.21.2) or let it run; skip time only by unanimous consent (Valheim sleep); leave the absent character's body in the world (Conan Exiles, ARK) or remove it; and, in text role-play communities, pick a fixed ratio of game time to real time and repair the inconsistencies by hand.

### Cited Findings

#### Neverwinter Nights persistent worlds (2002 onward)
- Player-run servers built with the toolkit are called "persistent worlds". — [Wikipedia: Persistent world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_world)
- Resting: players report that nearly all large servers restrict resting to particular places or impose a long cooldown outside safe zones. — [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/704450/discussions/0/3191362948564094622) (forum)
- Clock trouble: on a multi-server world, the calendar moving backwards when a module restarts made characters wait more than 24 game hours to rest and broke offline-rest calculations. A disconnected character's position is saved and restored unless the module has reset, in which case they return to the start. — [A Land Far Away forum: Time in a multiserver environment](https://www.alandfaraway.info/phpBB3/viewtopic.php?p=502672); [GOG forum](https://gog.com/forum/neverwinter_nights_series/a_few_neverwinter_nights_server_questions) (forum, snippets)

#### Text role-play (MUSH) time ratios
- A 2000 editorial for MUSH administrators compares fixed ratios of in-character to real time. At 1:1, dates are easy but long events (training, pregnancy) take real months and fast scenes outrun people who cannot be online. At 4 game days per real day, long plots resolve quickly but a scene that covers one evening in the fiction and is played over three real nights leaves the game 12 days out of step and needs retroactive fixes, and a player joining a scene late assumes the wrong time of day. At 1 game day per 4 real days scenes can be played at their natural pace with less retroactive play, but everything drags. The author concludes there is no best ratio. — [AresMUSH: IC vs RL Time (Faraday, 2000)](https://aresmush.com/articles/ic-vs-rl-time.html)

#### Minecraft (dedicated servers)
- Since Java Edition 1.21.2 the vanilla server has a `pause-when-empty-seconds` setting: after the server has been empty for that many seconds, world ticking stops. Sources conflict on the default (one reference lists -1, disabled; another shows 60 seconds). A widely used server container now recommends the native setting over its own older auto-pause. — [PaperMC docs: server.properties](https://docs.papermc.io/paper/reference/server-properties); [docker-minecraft-server docs: autopause](https://docker-minecraft-server.readthedocs.io/en/latest/misc/autopause-autostop/autopause/) (snippets)

#### Valheim
- A dedicated server keeps the world running when the hosting player logs off; nothing pauses for menus (smelters finish, raids fire). A mod adds pausing when exactly one player is online. One host's forum post says the server is "supposed to be paused" when empty yet still uses CPU. — [Supercraft hosting guide](https://supercraft.host/wiki/valheim/guide/); [Thunderstore: PauseMyServer mod](https://thunderstore.io/c/valheim/p/DeathMonger/PauseMyServer/); [TCAdmin forum](https://community.tcadmin.com/topic/11540-valheim-11/page/3/) (snippets; no official source)
- Skipping the night requires every online player to be in bed; until then time passes normally. Several popular mods exist solely to relax this to one sleeper or to a vote (default 50 percent). — [Thunderstore: NowYouSleep](https://thunderstore.io/c/valheim/p/Hex_Viking/NowYouSleep/); [Thunderstore: LessSleep](https://thunderstore.io/c/valheim/p/LessCx/LessSleep/); [Quarter to Three forum](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/valheim-a-viking-multiplayer-exploration-and-survival-game-by-coffee-stain/150991?page=26) (snippets)

#### Conan Exiles and ARK
- Conan Exiles: by default a logged-out character's body stays in the world unconscious (a server setting can remove it); a hosting guide gives 168 hours before an offline body despawns. Players report being killed and looted while offline. Official PvP servers use a raid-time window rather than offline protection. — [Conan Exiles wiki: Server Configuration](https://conanexiles.fandom.com/wiki/Server_Configuration); [Wabbanode hosting guide](https://wabbanode.com/help/conan-exiles/how-to-configure-death-and-corpse-settings-on-your-conan-exiles-server); [Funcom forums](https://forums.funcom.com/t/logged-out-character-in-base-was-killed-and-looted-items-in-inventory-lost-to-decay/238072) (snippets; last is forum)
- ARK: a logged-out character stays asleep in the world and can be killed by players or creatures. Official PvE servers moved to Offline Raid Protection in patch 510.0 (27 July 2017); on private servers it is an optional setting that makes a tribe's base invulnerable while the tribe is offline. — [ARK wiki: PS 510.0](https://ark.wiki.gg/wiki/PS_510.0); [BisectHosting help](https://help.bisecthosting.com/hc/en-us/articles/40328851817371) (snippets)

#### Project Gorgon
- "Hang outs": a player sets their character to spend time with an NPC, and the activity progresses while logged off, earning favor; the wiki says offline rewards accrue whether the absence is 30 minutes or 8 hours. — [Project Gorgon wiki: NPC quests and favors](https://wiki.projectgorgon.com/wiki/General_gameplay_tips/NPC%27s_Quests_and_Favors); [Massively OP starter guide](https://massivelyop.com/2016/04/16/massively-ops-guide-to-the-project-gorgon-starter-island/) (snippets)

#### Wurm Online
- Actions have real-time timers whose length depends on stamina and skill; the remaining time is shown in the event window, and a 2024 client change shows minutes and seconds for long actions. Actions can be queued: two queued plus one active below 30 Mind Logic, one more per 10 points, up to nine queued. — [Wurmpedia: Action](https://wurmpedia.com/index.php/Action); [Wurmpedia: Action queue](https://wurmpedia.com/index.php/Action_queue); [Wurmpedia: Mind logic](https://wurmpedia.com/index.php/Ml)

#### Haven and Hearth
- The hearth fire is the character's home point: where they appear on login if their logout spot is not accessible, and a fast-travel destination ("hearthing") at a small travel-weariness cost. Hearthing is blocked in combat, while swimming, inside a dungeon and while "Red Handed" after a crime. The flame's color shows whether the owner is online, and inspecting it shows when they last logged in. — [Ring of Brodgar (Haven and Hearth wiki): Hearth Fire](https://ringofbrodgar.com/wiki/Hearth_Fire)

#### RuneScape (Old School)
- Logout is refused until 16 game ticks (9.6 seconds, so 0.6 seconds a tick) after last being hit. A character that disconnects in combat stays in the world for 60 seconds. Idle players are logged out after 5 minutes (configurable up to 30) and everyone is logged out after 6 hours of continuous play. Switching worlds needs 10 seconds out of combat. — [Old School RuneScape wiki: Logout button](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/Logout_button)

### Inferences
- Minecraft adding a native pause for empty servers in 1.21.2 (after years of third-party auto-pause tools) is evidence that for small groups the default expectation is "nothing happens to my world while none of us is there". Valheim's lack of an equivalent is what the pause mods are for. For a host-authoritative game the natural rule is that the game clock advances only as a consequence of player actions, which gives the pause for free.
- Valheim's sleep rule is the simplest working example of a group advancing a shared clock by consent, and its mods show how it fails: unanimity stops working as soon as one player is busy elsewhere, so servers move to "any one sleeper" or a vote. With no cap on players, any rule that needs every online player's agreement to pass time will stall; a rule based on each character being individually "busy until" a time scales better.
- The MUSH editorial is the most directly relevant source found for a text game: it documents, from practice, that with a fixed ratio scenes fall out of step with the calendar and late joiners assume the wrong time, and that communities patch this by hand. A game with an engine that owns the clock can do what MUSH staff could not: record the game time of every scene and enforce an ordering.
- Sleeping bodies (Conan, ARK) make absence dangerous and are a constant source of complaints even in games built around raiding. For co-op, leaving an absent character present in the world is only attractive if the engine can also protect or run them; otherwise UO's inn rule or Haven and Hearth's home point is the better precedent.
- Haven and Hearth's rule "appear where you logged out if that is still possible, otherwise at your home point" is a clean fallback for the case where the world changed around an absent character (the building burned, the party moved on).
- Project Gorgon's hang outs are a declared offline activity: the player states before leaving what the character will be doing. This transfers directly to a text game as a downtime declaration, and answers "what was my character doing while I was away" without simulation.
- Wurm's queued long actions show that players will accept very long activities if they can line up several and let them run; in a text game the queue collapses to a single action whose cost is game time.
- The Neverwinter Nights reports show the practical bug class to guard against: any stored "last rested at" or "busy until" timestamp breaks if the clock can ever go backwards or differ between parts of the world. One monotonic game clock owned by the host avoids it.

### Gaps
- Neverwinter Nights server time ratios: the Arelith wiki page on time returned 403 and no figure was sourced. Not reported.
- Valheim: no official statement on whether a dedicated server's day counter advances when empty; the evidence is a hosting guide, mod descriptions and one forum remark that point in different directions.
- Haven and Hearth: what happens to a character's body at logout, and any logout timer, were not found; two searches returned nothing specific. The commonly repeated claim that characters who log out after crimes or in combat remain vulnerable is unverified here.
- Wurm Online: no source found for typical or maximum action durations, or for anything progressing while offline other than sleep bonus (section 6).
- ARK: whether official servers still run is disputed in the sources (one wiki says they closed 29 September 2023 with the move to the successor game).
- Conan Exiles, ARK and Valheim claims rest on fan wikis, hosting-company guides and forums; no developer documentation was opened.

## 6. Rested experience and other ways of turning time away into a benefit

### Takeaway
Rested experience began as a penalty for long sessions in World of Warcraft's beta and shipped as the same numbers presented as a bonus for time away; the reframing, not the arithmetic, fixed the reception. Variants either bank a capped bonus that is spent on return (WoW, Wurm) or let the character do something declared while the player is gone (EVE training, Project Gorgon, SWG harvesters, idle games).

### Cited Findings
- Origin: in WoW's beta, experience gain fell the longer a player stayed on, to nudge people to stop after a couple of hours; players hated it. As relayed from Rob Pardo, the team doubled the experience needed for everything and then presented the first stretch after a break as a 200 percent "rested" rate decaying to a normal 100 percent. The numbers were the same; players now liked it. The account is secondhand (Chris Remo on a podcast, quoted by a psychologist's blog). — [The Psychology of Video Games: Framing and WoW's rest system (2010)](https://www.psychologyofgames.com/2010/03/framing-and-world-of-warcrafts-rest-system/)
- The same story appears in a long history of WoW's development, with Pardo saying nonstop play was not healthy and the team wanted to prevent burnout; a wiki records that the beta had additional negative states (fatigued, exhausted) that were dropped, and quotes Blizzard describing the shipped system as the same numbers seen from the other side. — [USgamer: How World of Warcraft Was Made](https://www.usgamer.net/articles/how-world-of-warcraft-was-made-the-inside-story); [Warcraft wiki: Resting](https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Resting) (snippets)
- The blog's explanation is framing and loss aversion: a late fee and an early-bird discount are the same price, felt differently. — [The Psychology of Video Games (2010)](https://www.psychologyofgames.com/2010/03/framing-and-world-of-warcrafts-rest-system/)
- Wurm Online's sleep bonus: logging out in a bed earns 1 minute of bonus per 24 real minutes asleep (minimum one hour; about an hour of bonus per day away), with lesser beds giving 75 percent. The store is capped (base 5 hours; the page gives conflicting higher caps up to 10). While active it doubles skill gain and triples stamina recovery, and the player can freeze and unfreeze it at will. — [Wurmpedia: Sleep bonus](https://wurmpedia.com/index.php/Sleep_bonus)
- Counter-example: Star Wars Galaxies' battle fatigue did not clear during time logged out, which its designer later named as part of why it was resented. — [Raph Koster, "Forcing interaction" (2005)](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=198)
- Declared-activity variants: EVE skills train while offline — [EVE University wiki](https://wiki.eveuniversity.org/Skills_and_learning); Project Gorgon hang outs progress while logged off — [Project Gorgon wiki](https://wiki.projectgorgon.com/wiki/General_gameplay_tips/NPC%27s_Quests_and_Favors) (snippet); A Tale in the Desert's "Offline Flax" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Tale_in_the_Desert).

### Inferences
- Three properties recur in the well-received versions: the benefit is tied to where the character was left (inn, bed), it is capped, and it is spent by playing rather than replacing play. Wurm adds player control over when it is spent.
- For a co-op game where sessions and attendance are uneven, the banked-and-capped form doubles as a catch-up mechanism for the friend who missed a week, without the engine having to invent what their character did. The declared-activity form answers that question explicitly instead. The two can coexist: a default ("rested at the inn") and an option ("spent the time training with the smith").
- Because the target game advances time by actions, any rested benefit must be keyed to something a present player cannot farm by repeatedly waiting; keying it to the absent player's real absence, or capping it tightly, follows the idle-game and Wurm precedents.

### Gaps
- No primary Blizzard source (a talk or post by Pardo himself) was opened; the origin story is consistent across sources but secondhand.
- Variants in other games (Final Fantasy XIV sanctuaries, The Lord of the Rings Online, and others) were not researched.
- No data on how players received Wurm's sleep bonus.

## 7. Design writing: Koster, Bartle, and what was and was not found

### Takeaway
Koster's position, refined after Star Wars Galaxies, is that downtime is necessary for social play but must fall at natural troughs and cannot be produced by forcing people to wait or group; Bartle supplies the vocabulary (a world that continues when you are gone; world persistence versus persistence of events). The specific academic literature on one-time events in shared worlds was not located.

### Cited Findings
- Koster, "Forcing interaction" (9 December 2005): attention in a game rises and falls; socializing competes for the same attention, so it happens in the troughs ("socialization requires downtime"). Forced grouping produces teamwork at the peaks, not conversation. Travel is a middle state, neither peak nor trough. Proximity matters (the hubs in Asheron's Call were popular because they were convenient), and bars and other "third places" built for low-intensity activity are better models than corridors. Battle fatigue was mistimed, not wrong in principle. In comments he suggests WoW feels casual because its activities come in small pieces with frequent troughs, and that logging in and out are themselves troughs. — [Raph Koster, "Forcing interaction"](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=198)
- Koster's SWG postmortem: the team wanted places players chose to spend downtime; a research study found the cantinas did not work as intended as third places though the game as a whole did; some of the social loop was simply forced. — [Raph Koster, "Designing a Living Society in SWG, part two" (2015)](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=25743)
- Koster elsewhere says designers are always either forcing or inhibiting interaction, and in a 2003 mailing-list reply treats travel time as a problem when players can find a way to shortcut it. — [Raph Koster, "Ways to make your virtual space more social" (2009)](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=2486); [MUD-Dev archive (2003)](https://mud-dev.zer7.com/2003/11/28710) (snippets)
- Bartle's definition, as quoted from *Designing Virtual Worlds* (2003): a persistent world continues to exist and develop internally even when nobody is interacting with it. The encyclopedia article built on it separates world persistence (the world is there whenever you log in) from game persistence (events carry over), with a "Groundhog Day" world that resets as the counter-case, and adds "pseudo-persistence" (data restored when a player reconnects to an instance) and scheduled access (the world is only up at set times). It records that MUD1 (1978) reset every 105 minutes and that most early worlds kept only scores between resets. — [Wikipedia: Persistent world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_world)
- Bartle, "Why Virtual Worlds are Designed by Newbies" (2004): worlds depend on a flow of newcomers who judge by their first world and reject features that are good for the long term but uncomfortable in the short term; a contemporaneous discussion quotes him predicting instancing would become locked in as an expectation. — [Game Developer reprint](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/soapbox-why-virtual-worlds-are-designed-by-newbies---no-really-); [MUD-Dev archive (2004)](https://mud-dev.zer7.com/2004/11/31166) (snippets)
- An academic critique argues that Bartle's later, shorter definition implies only shared time and space and misses what makes a world a world. — [arXiv 1511.08464](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.08464.pdf) (snippet)
- Practitioner commentary on one-time events: the 2009 EVE column (few can attend; make events long and multi-site) — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2009-06-07-eve-evolved-world-events-in-eve-online.html); a blogger's economic reading of downtime (it stretched content and lost customers) — [Tobold's blog (2006)](https://tobolds.blogspot.com/2006/06/changes-part-3-time.html?m=1).

### Inferences
- Koster's distinction separates two things that older games fused: time cost in the fiction, and time cost to the player. Boats, shuttles and meditation charged the player real minutes in order to create troughs for conversation among strangers. A text game for friends who are already talking has no need to manufacture troughs, so travel and recovery can cost game time (with all the world consequences that implies) while costing the player nothing but a line of narration.
- Bartle's two kinds of persistence map onto two separate design decisions for a small world: whether the world exists when no one is connected (it can pause without ceasing to be persistent in the second sense) and whether events stick (the requirement here). The target game needs game persistence strongly and world persistence only weakly.
- Bartle's "newbie" argument explains why large games drifted toward instancing and reversible events even where designers preferred permanence. A game played by a fixed group of friends is free of that pressure.

## 8. For a world shared by a handful of friends: which mechanisms solve problems of scale, and which transfer?

### Takeaway
Almost everything invented after 2004 (megaservers, channels, phasing, per-player story and loot, reversible events, travel throttles) exists to manage crowds of strangers and should be left behind; what transfers comes mostly from the oldest and the smallest worlds: a safe place to leave a character, long activities as jobs with an end time on one clock, time skipped by consent or by individual busy-until times, lazy catch-up, capped benefits for time away, and a durable record of one-time events.

### Cited Findings
(Each item restates a sourced fact from above that the judgment rests on.)
- Phasing separates grouped friends at different quest stages, with no indicator. — [UESP: Megaservers](https://en.uesp.net/wiki/Online:Megaservers) (snippet); [ESO forum](https://forums.elderscrollsonline.com/en/discussion/161407/friend-in-group-disappearing) (forum)
- Per-timeline phasing leaves family members unable to play together. — [Blizzard forum](https://us.forums.blizzard.com/en/wow/t/i-cant-play-with-my-lower-level-family-members-stuck-in-chromie-time/1972491) (forum)
- Instanced story spaces let teammates watch but not share choices or progress. — [Wccftech review of Wastelanders](https://wccftech.com/review/fallout-76-wastelanders-country-roads-take-me-home/) (snippet)
- Repeating events make consequences ultimately temporary. — [Guild Wars 2 wiki: Dynamic event](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Dynamic_event)
- Temporary story content was later rebuilt as permanent, replayable episodes. — [Guild Wars 2 wiki: Living World Season 1](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Living_World_Season_1)
- A one-place, one-time event reached under a hundred of 300,000 players. — [Engadget (2009)](https://www.engadget.com/2009-06-07-eve-evolved-world-events-in-eve-online.html)

### Inferences

Mechanisms that exist only because of scale (do not transfer):
- Shards, megaservers, channels, layers, overflow maps, the 32-player instance: capacity management. With one host and a few friends there is one world.
- Phasing, per-player quest state, per-player loot, solo story instances: these give each of thousands of players a private copy of consequences. They directly contradict "the world changes permanently in response to players" and are the source of the best-documented complaints (cannot see or help a friend).
- Reversible dynamic events and respawning content: needed so that anonymous latecomers find something to do. In a small persistent world a retaken fort should stay retaken until something in the fiction changes it.
- Jump fatigue, combat log-off timers, house decay, camp bonuses as anti-crowding devices, raid windows: all regulate competition among strangers.
- Forced waits (boats, shuttles, meditation, cantina visits): built to create conversation among strangers and to stretch content; the evidence is that players resented paying real minutes and that the market moved away from it. Friends in a text game are already in conversation.
- Seasonal vaulting and time-limited story: a production-cost problem of live service games.

Mechanisms that transfer, and how:
- A defined place and state for an absent character (UO inns and houses; Haven and Hearth's home point with fallback). The rule to copy is "where you stop determines how safely you are gone, and if the world changed, you reappear at home".
- Long activities as jobs on one clock (EVE skills and industry, SWG harvesters, Wurm queues), with the game clock in place of the wall clock. Each character is "busy until T"; effects are applied when the clock passes T, and optionally only take hold when the owner is next present.
- One world simulation advanced in discrete steps and applied once for everyone (Elite's tick), independent of which sub-party's actions moved the clock.
- A rule for skipping time. Precedents: unanimous consent (Valheim), which breaks down as the group grows; per-character busy-until times (EVE), which do not need consent; and adopting the authoritative clock of the place you join (Animal Crossing visits). For split parties in one world, the combination suggested by the evidence is a single monotonic clock, per-character busy-until times, and reunions that occur at the later time with the earlier party deemed to have waited. No surveyed game lets two groups sit at different times in the same place without the visibility failures reported for Chromie Time.
- Lazy catch-up on return (Animal Crossing, idle games): compute what would have happened across the gap instead of simulating it live. The world pauses when nobody is connected (Minecraft) but "keeps moving" in the sense that elapsed game time is accounted for when play resumes.
- Capped, place-tied benefits for time away, and declared downtime activities (rested experience, Wurm sleep bonus, Project Gorgon hang outs). These double as catch-up for the friend who missed sessions.
- Protection of an absent player's stake (EVE reinforcement windows, ARK offline protection): the world may threaten what an absent player owns, but should not irreversibly resolve it without a window in which they can act.
- Durable records of one-time events (the Thistledown monument; Guild Wars 2's story journal). In a world of permanent change every event is one-time by design; the lesson of the large games is that the failure is not the event being unrepeatable but the latecomer having no account of it.
- Shared milestones that the whole group advances (A Tale in the Desert's Tests; Asheron's Call's monthly arc), with authorial steering kept in reserve as Turbine did.

Specific tensions with the stated requirements:
- "One typed action can consume seconds or days" has no precedent in the large real-time games, all of which tie game time to wall time. The closest precedents are tabletop-derived text role-play (the MUSH editorial) and consent-based skips. The report should treat this as the point where MMO practice runs out and other researchers' material (tabletop and play-by-post conventions) must carry the argument.
- "No cap on players" argues against any mechanism requiring unanimity (sleep votes) and for mechanisms that are per-character (busy-until, declared downtime) or per-place (join the local clock).
- "Players can split up and reunite" is exactly the case phasing handles worst at scale. The safe precedent is Guild Wars 1's structure (shared hub, separate excursions) made honest by having both happen in one world state on one clock.

### Gaps
- No surveyed online game combines a single persistent shared world with action-driven (rather than wall-clock) time for multiple simultaneous players; these notes found no direct precedent, and the absence may reflect the limits of this search rather than a true absence.
- Player-reception evidence in these notes is mostly forum posts and blogs, flagged where used; no survey or telemetry data was found for any mechanism.
- Sources not reached that would likely strengthen this section: Bartle's book (chapters on time and persistence), GDC talks on megaservers and phasing, Massively Overpowered's design roundtables, and any academic treatment of unrepeatable events in shared worlds.
